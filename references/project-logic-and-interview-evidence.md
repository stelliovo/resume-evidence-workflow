# Project Logic and Problem-Solving Evidence

Use this reference for substantial experiences containing several projects, workstreams, or operational changes. It lightly adapts project and program logic; it is not a claim of strict PMBOK, PRINCE2, or formal evaluation compliance.

## Method basis

The structure draws on:

- business-case and project-charter thinking: connect work to the underlying need, intended value, scope, assumptions, and constraints;
- logic models: distinguish need, activities, direct outputs, and outcomes;
- theory of change: examine the assumed links between actions and outcomes instead of treating sequence as proof of causality;
- benefits realization: check what value actually appeared, continued, or was reused after delivery.

Useful primary sources:

- [PMI: Delivering project benefits](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/delivering-project-benefits-model-overview-6904)
- [PMI: The need for a business case](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/need-business-case-6730)
- [CDC: Describe the program and develop a logic model](https://www.cdc.gov/evaluation/php/evaluation-framework-action-guide/step-2-describe-the-program.html)
- [UK Government Analysis Function: Theory of Change toolkit](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/the-analysis-function-theory-of-change-toolkit/)

## Lightweight evidence chain

Use this internally:

```text
need or opportunity
→ intended objective
→ user and team activities
→ direct outputs
→ observed outcomes
→ longer-term use, benefit, or learning
```

Context, constraints, assumptions, other contributors, and external factors may affect any link.

Do not force every field into the user-facing bank. Inputs, budgets, schedules, risks, or stakeholder maps matter only when they clarify the work, support a target JD, or reveal a distinctive contribution.

## Experience-level and project-level reasons

Record two levels when applicable:

1. **Overall background and objective:** the shared problem or goal explaining why several workstreams existed.
2. **Project trigger:** the specific event, observation, request, or problem that caused one project to start.

Distinguish:

- an objective explicitly stated by the team at the time;
- the user's understanding at the time;
- the user's retrospective interpretation;
- an Agent inference.

Only the first three may enter the factual bank with their correct attribution. Do not convert an Agent inference into fact.

## Outputs, outcomes, and attribution

- **Activity:** what the user or team did.
- **Direct output:** the immediate product, such as an event, document, account, workflow, analysis, prototype, or database.
- **Outcome:** what changed afterward for users, operations, decisions, revenue, quality, speed, or behavior.
- **Benefit or continuation:** whether the change persisted, scaled, was reused, or created later value.

Do not equate an output with an outcome. Do not claim that an outcome was caused solely by the user when the evidence shows contribution, team ownership, or external factors.

Record expected and actual outcomes separately. Failed or partial projects remain useful evidence when they reveal judgment, adaptation, boundaries, or learning.

## Problem-solving evidence without database bloat

The evidence bank should preserve how the user handled meaningful situations without becoming a library of finished interview answers.

Store a compact key-event card only when an event contains meaningful tension or judgment:

```markdown
#### Event name
- Situation and people involved:
- What became difficult or uncertain:
- The user's responsibility:
- What the user did:
- Why the user chose that approach:
- What happened:
- Whether the team or user later reviewed the work:
- Improvements identified or actually applied afterward:
- Useful question themes:
```

Typical signals include disagreement, influence without authority, ambiguity, failure, learning, prioritization, pressure, customer handling, risk, and initiative. Keep one to three strong events per experience rather than one event per category.

The resume may use only an event's outcome. Preserve the process fields so the user still understands what happened and how they handled it. This is enrichment of the evidence bank, not interview preparation.

## Reusable problem-solving methods

When the user has formed a repeatable way of working, store it as a compact method linked to a real project. This helps the user recognize their own approach and keeps the bank useful beyond a single resume.

Do not write generic methods into the bank merely because they sound correct. The method must be recoverable from the user's actual decisions and actions.
