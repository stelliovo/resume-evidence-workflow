# Question Selection and Stop Rules

## Preview the module

Before deep-diving an experience, give the user a rough, non-binding expectation such as `about 5–8 core questions` and name the likely themes. Adjust the estimate to the experience's complexity. State directly that one complete answer may resolve several themes and reduce follow-ups; do not add scripted language intended to create a casual atmosphere.

The preview is orientation, not a quota or promise. Do not tell the user that no question will ever be repeated.

## Choose the next question

Before asking, identify its expected information gain. Prefer questions that may reveal a new capability or business branch, distinguish individual from team work, uncover a decision or adaptation, establish evidence, resolve a material conflict, or make a future claim defensible. If none applies, defer the question.

Check the current answer, earlier discussion of the same experience, existing evidence records, and materially similar experiences before asking. Keep this screening internal.

## Three question classes

### Discovery

May open a new branch. Ask these first when the experience has only one apparent storyline: monetization, governance, customer handling, partnerships, process creation, failure, adoption, or reuse.

For substantial work containing multiple projects, also establish the causal context:

- What happened that led the team to start this work?
- What was the collection of projects trying to change or achieve?
- Why did each major project begin when it did?
- Were the projects responses to one shared problem or separate needs?

Ask these in natural, concrete language. Do not use `business case`, `logic model`, or `benefits realization` unless the user already uses those terms or asks for the framework.

### Deepening

Strengthens a promising known branch. Ask for the user's action, reasoning, constraints, evidence, outcome, and boundary. Do not mechanically demand every STAR field. Some useful evidence has no quantified result.

### Administrative

Exact meeting counts, minor tool choices, exhaustive collaborator lists, or low-value chronological details. Ask only when they affect verification, interpretation, or immediate output.

## Cross-experience transfer

When two experiences share a workflow, audience, account type, organization, or operating model, identify common facts once and ask mainly about meaningful differences.

- Apply a shared fact across experiences only when the user explicitly says it is shared.
- Use a fact from one experience as a memory cue for another only when the second context makes it plausible and the answer could reveal a valuable branch.
- Do not automatically transfer a negative fact merely because two experiences look similar.
- Do not ask the same low-value screening question platform by platform when the user has already described one shared operating model.
- If two successive prompts receive “same as the previous one,” “already covered,” or an equivalent answer, stop the mechanical scan and reassess.

For example, if two fictional campus newsletters were described as one shared editorial operation and the user already confirmed neither accepted sponsorship, do not recheck monetization publication by publication. If only one publication's commercial activity is known and the other differs materially in audience or business model, one brief comparison question may still be useful.

## Depth test

Continue only if the next answer could change the capability label, whether the branch deserves a bullet, claim strength or safety, ownership boundary, evidence type, problem-solving value, or downstream role relevance.

## Stop conditions

Stop or defer when:

- two successive answers add detail but no new signal;
- the branch repeats evidence already captured elsewhere;
- the user cannot retrieve the information and precision is unnecessary;
- the branch is neither differentiating nor likely to affect future use;
- continued questioning would mainly satisfy completeness;
- user fatigue makes a checkpoint more valuable than another question.

Completion means all meaningful experience modules have been surfaced and scanned for distinct value dimensions, not that every detail has been recorded. Maintain a deferred list with a reason such as `job-description-specific`, `low information gain`, `needs source`, or `not currently retrievable`. Do not turn it into an automatic future questionnaire.

## Problem-solving event scan

After the main business facts of an experience are clear, perform at most one short event scan when the experience is likely to contain a meaningful example of how the user handled a problem. Choose only the most plausible directions from the context, such as disagreement, unclear direction, failure, persuasion, limited time or resources, competing priorities, negative feedback, risk, or unplanned responsibility.

Use one broad natural-language prompt rather than asking every behavioral category separately. If no event emerges, stop. Do not force every experience to produce a behavioral story.

Store only a compact event card. Do not turn it into STAR/CAR preparation inside this skill.

## Language adaptation

Use professional judgment internally and accessible language externally.

- Default to clear, specific, neutral wording rather than either slang or résumé jargon.
- Mirror terminology that the user already uses accurately.
- Translate an abstract category into an observable scene when needed: `Did more customers come afterward?` instead of `Did this drive growth?`; `Did anyone use this method again?` instead of `Was the mechanism reused?`.
- Do not reduce the depth of the question when simplifying its wording.
- Do not infer a user's preferred register from education, title, or age. Infer cautiously from their language and responses, and follow explicit preferences.

After a substantial answer, provide concise feedback using slightly more professional, resume-adjacent language to show what information was extracted. Present it directly rather than announcing a framework or labeling `facts`, `capabilities`, or `provisional wording`. Usually one short paragraph or a few bullets is enough.

Do not add an explanation of how the feedback will become future resume language. Do not produce a full rewritten bullet after every answer. Keep the underlying evidence bank neutral, and reserve final positioning and bullet decisions for the job description (JD) tailoring stage.
